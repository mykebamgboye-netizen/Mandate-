Give the country its own desk: a stamp answers the person who asked, and the world hears it the Monday after.

Before this patch, the only thing that could move the living world was time — 250 actors proposing a week each, the matrix drifting, the polls closing — and the desk could stamp papers without affecting a single man in the country. Now a stamp on an approach is read back as an answer to the person who sent it, landed on both sides of the relationship before that actor's Monday is run, and written to his own file. A bribe paid actually leaves the patronage bank and is written back into the strip; a leak the desk declines to buy back runs as a clipping in the press folder and takes publicly visible points off the desk's approval. A receipt paper, filed on Monday, names what every stamp did and how far it moved each man, and says that a paper left unstamped writes nothing — so the player is told, on the sheet, what each stamp means.

One real defect this closes is that the country only ever addressed the desk with money. The brain looked every actor up in the NPC index, which does not contain the player, and skipped the desk's own edges — so four of the five kinds of approach never arrived, and the living world's bills to the desk were a single noiseless type of ask. The brain now treats the player as an actor it can name and count its standing against.

Also adds the country's own register in a form the desk can test and the player can read: `closeLivingWeek` does, in one call, open the world, read the closing tray as answers, run one Monday, write it back, and hand back the papers, the press, the purse and the standing. The desk's week close calls exactly that one function, so the path the player takes and the path the tests take are the same one; and every living paper carries its own `ask` on the sheet, so a stamp on it is read back without parsing the prose.

A kind that was typed too narrowly gets corrected: press stories are now signed, so the press folder's "NET POLLING" actually adds up the two directions a paper can move, with the desktop's defending leak now printing on the player's own seat rather than on a stranger's name.

Finally, a stack of loose ends the century hides behind: a lost seat is impossible because the desk re-seats the player every close; the world can sell a seat but never give one; the desk's own acts never touch a named actor; and the country outgrows its save at roughly twelve kilobytes a week, with a five-megabyte guarded write that simply drops the week and says nothing. Read `src/notes/015-living-desk-audit.txt` for the full list and the numbers, and `src/living-desk.ts` for the contract that the desk and the tests both walk.
